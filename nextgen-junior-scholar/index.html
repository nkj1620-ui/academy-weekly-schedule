A different kind of learning

From curiosity to meaningful contribution

This short, intensive project invites Grade 7–8 students to investigate a real societal issue, listen to expert perspectives, develop an evidence-informed solution, and share their learning with families. Students are researchers, collaborators, designers, and communicators—not passive workshop participants.

Pilot proposal — not an announced or confirmed mentor event. December dates, mentor participation, guest affiliations, and instructor availability are tentative. No university or hospital sponsorship, endorsement, formal partnership, or mentor appointment is implied.
Learning approach

Design Thinking

Empathize → Define → Ideate → Prototype → Test & Reflect. Students learn to formulate a responsible problem statement and evaluate who benefits from their ideas.

Mentorship model

Learn from professionals

Two or three proposed live online meetings with a relevant professor or practitioner, including an introduction, feedback on developing ideas, and final questions or review.

Choose one problem space

Proposed mentor-led tracks

TRACK A · TECHNOLOGY & SOCIETY

Technology for Human Well-Being

Potential mentor: CMU-affiliated IT professor, subject to direct invitation and confirmation.

Inquiry question: How could responsible technology help solve a problem in our school or local community?

  • Digital accessibility, online safety, information gaps, technology and equity
  • Student outputs: low-fidelity app prototype, user journey, policy proposal or demonstrator
  • Mentor sessions: problem framing → solution critique → reflection (2–3 proposed)
TRACK B · HEALTH & SOCIETY

Community Well-Being and Trauma-Informed Support

Potential mentor: Maria, a proposed Harvard Medical School/MGH-related faculty contact; exact title and affiliation require verification and consent.

Inquiry question: How might a school or community create safer, more supportive environments for young people?

  • Belonging, stress literacy, supportive environments and responsible public communication
  • Student outputs: awareness campaign, resource guide, inclusive-space concept or policy brief
  • Mentor sessions: public-health foundations → feedback → reflection (2–3 proposed)

This is an educational public-health design track, not clinical trauma training or counseling. Students should not conduct trauma interviews, collect sensitive health histories, or give medical advice.

December 2026 · Proposed schedule

Three weeks, one meaningful outcome

01

Discover & Define

In-person · English-speaking instructor

Introduce design thinking, choose a meaningful question, examine credible evidence, and practice research ethics and interview etiquette.

  • Team roles & project charter
  • Problem statement and stakeholder map
  • Research notes & mentor questions
02

Mentorship & Prototype

Online · invited expert

Attend 2–3 proposed online mentor workshops, subject to schedules and availability. Between meetings, students conduct supervised research and build a draft solution.

  • Expert Q&A and feedback log
  • Evidence-based prototype or proposal
  • Revision after mentor feedback
03

Refine & Present

In-person · English-speaking instructor

Complete a public-facing communication product and rehearse a structured team presentation for families.

  • Final video, slide deck or paper
  • Reflection and source attribution
  • Parent showcase & Q&A

Suggested operations: one primary mentor track per team, rather than expecting both sets of guests in three weeks. The instructor facilitates throughout the project, even during the online week. Scheduling should accommodate mentor time zones and instructor travel.

Evidence of learning

Student-led final showcase

Each team selects one main format and also prepares a short live presentation with questions from parents.

Short Documentary / Video

3–5 minutes explaining the problem, evidence, student ideas, and a realistic next step.

Presentation / Prototype

8–12 slides, with a process map, user needs, solution prototype and limitations.

Research Paper / Policy Brief

Approximately 800–1,200 words with citations, recommendation, counterargument and reflection.

All teams should submit: a problem statement, at least three credible sources, design-process evidence, mentor feedback notes (where permitted), a revision log, a final artifact, and an individual reflection. Proposed showcase format: 7–10-minute team presentation plus 3–5-minute parent Q&A.

Quality standards

What makes a Junior Scholar project credible?

Learning outcomes

  • Use reliable sources, distinguish evidence from opinion
  • Explain a problem from more than one stakeholder perspective
  • Develop a feasible and ethical prototype
  • Communicate clearly in academic English
  • Revise in response to evidence and feedback

Mentor & student protection

  • Confirm guest names, roles and consent before promotion
  • Obtain parental consent for minors' online meetings and recording
  • Have a NexGen teacher present and moderate all sessions
  • Do not publish students' names, faces, personal stories or health information without explicit permission
  • Use only teacher-created summaries or properly licensed materials

For families

Big questions deserve thoughtful answers.

Junior Scholar students will not be judged by whether they invent a perfect solution in three weeks. They will be evaluated on the quality of their questions, depth of research, empathy for others, responsiveness to expert feedback, teamwork, and clarity of communication.
